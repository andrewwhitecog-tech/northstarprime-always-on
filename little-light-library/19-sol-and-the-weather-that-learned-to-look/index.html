SIGNAL 01 THE WEATHER FOLD Celestine Fold hung between a blue sky above and a silver sea of clouds below. At its center stood the Sky Loom: a round brass wheel with six clear crystal vanes. Weather-keepers played tones through the wheel, and the clouds carried sun, mist, wind, and rain to the floating gardens. Sol was the youngest weather-keeper. He was Deaf. He felt deep notes through his cyan boots, watched faces closely, and signed with quick violet-gloved hands. The tower had been built for ears. Sol had learned to notice what ears sometimes missed.

SIGNAL 02 WHEN SOUND VANISHED On Rainflower Day, six cloud bands were supposed to water six thirsty gardens. Then electric fog rolled over the city. The weather-keepers sounded the Sky Loom. Their tones flashed through the brass floor - but the fog swallowed every note before it reached the clouds. One cloud drifted toward the library. Another sank over the bakery chimneys. Four wandered in uncertain circles. The keepers played louder. Sol shook his head. "Louder cannot carry through a wall," he signed to his mother. "The message needs another way."

SIGNAL 03 WHAT THE CLOUDS WATCHED Sol stepped onto the highest terrace and lifted one orange sleeve. The nearest cloud turned its bright edge toward him. He raised his prism baton and traced one slow curve through the air. The cloud followed. Sol stopped halfway. The cloud stopped too. He completed the curve. The cloud rose exactly one terrace. The clouds were not waiting for a louder sound. They were watching for a complete signal. "So am I," Sol said.

SIGNAL 04 SOL'S FIRST SIGNAL Sol climbed beside the Sky Loom. He held the prism baton over his heart until every keeper looked at him. Then he drew a bright cyan curve upward. One cloud rose. The keepers cheered and turned toward one another before the curve was finished. The cloud wobbled. It did not know whether the message had ended. Sol lowered the baton. "First, find the eyes that will receive the signal," he signed. "Then hold the message long enough to be understood." This time, everyone kept looking.

SIGNAL 05 SIX SKY SIGNS Sol fitted six new crystal vanes into the Sky Loom. Each vane had a color, a shape, and a raised edge that fingers could feel. Cyan curved upward. Violet spiraled inward. Sea-green stretched sideways. Deep blue fell in gentle lines. Amber opened like a warm circle. White spread into a quiet six-point star. "Color may be missed," Sol explained. "Shape may be missed. A vibration may be missed. Together, they give the message more chances to arrive." The six vanes waited like a bright new alphabet with no letters.

SIGNAL 06 TOO FAST TO SEE The head keeper spun all six vanes at once. Cyan rose through violet. Amber flashed across deep blue. Sea-green cut through white. The clouds twisted into a knot that looked like six enormous socks in a washing bowl. Sol laughed, then stopped the wheel. "A message can be bright and still be confusing," he said. "One clear thing at a time." He returned every vane to its resting place. The clouds untangled themselves with six embarrassed little puffs.

SIGNAL 07 WAIT FOR MY EYES Sol paired the weather-keepers across the terrace. One keeper waited. The other looked up. Only then did the first keeper move a vane. They held each signal. They watched for an answering gesture. They changed places and tried again. At first, the pauses felt enormous. Then the city became strangely precise. No one guessed. No one rushed ahead. No one had to pretend a message had arrived when it had not. "A pause is not empty," Sol signed. "It is where understanding catches up."

SIGNAL 08 MORE THAN ONE WAY The keepers rebuilt the Sky Loom together. Its six vanes shone with color and shape. Low pulses traveled through the floor. Hand signals passed from terrace to terrace. Gem lanterns repeated each pattern through the fog. No signal had to do every job. No person had to receive every message in the same way. Sol pressed one glove to the warm brass and felt the whole system answer. Good weather-keeping was not one voice speaking louder. It was one message finding more than one door.

SIGNAL 09 THE STORM'S TURN Before the practice ended, the electric fog split open. An aurora storm rushed through the gap. Six cloud bands rolled toward Celestine Fold at once. Sol raised his baton. Cyan: rise. Violet: gather. Sea-green: cross. One by one, the keepers repeated each sign in light, vibration, and movement. They waited for the clouds to answer before sending the next. The six wild bands slowed, turned, and formed a wide circle around the city.

SIGNAL 10 RAIN IN THE RIGHT PLACES Deep blue fell in gentle lines. Six clouds released six curtains of rain. Water reached the moonmint beds, the hanging orchards, the glass-root greenhouse, the rooftop moss, the seed library, and the smallest nursery garden at the edge of the Fold. Amber warmed the air. White opened into a quiet star. The rain stopped. Every bridge stayed dry. Every cistern filled. Not one garden was forgotten. Below the city, the silver cloud sea carried a perfect ring of rainbow light.

SIGNAL 11 A CITY THAT LOOKED FIRST Rainflower Day began at last. Before calling across a table, neighbors found one another's eyes. Lantern sellers added flashes to their greetings. Drummers set low rhythms into the floor. Children invented silent weather dances beneath the six bright vanes. The new signals did not belong only to Sol. They belonged to anyone who had ever missed a word, entered a noisy room, stood behind thick glass, or needed one more moment. Sol watched the city look before it spoke. For once, he did not have to chase the message. The message came to meet him.

SIGNAL 12 THE RECEIVING RULE At dawn, Sol checked each colored vane and folded his prism baton into its case. Above the Sky Loom, the weather-keepers hung three new rules: Look for the receiver. Send the message more than one way. Wait for an answer. Outside, six cloud bands rested above six watered gardens. The weather had not grown ears. Sol had not needed fixing. Celestine Fold had simply learned that a message was not complete when it was sent. It was complete when someone could receive it. And from that morning on, even the weather learned to look.
